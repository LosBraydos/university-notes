Almost all common amino acids in proteins occur in the L-configuration. Less than 1 % are found in the D-configuration, often serving a precise structural purpose and is introduced by enzyme-catalyzed reactions ***after*** protein synthesis.
Asymmetric active sites of enzymes make it possible for cells to synthesize exclusively L-isomers.
Nine amino acids are essential (not synthesized in humans, supplied from diet). These are: M, I, L, K, F, T, W, V, H (Milk FTW, Very Heavy)
#### Nonpolar, aliphatic R groups
Hydrophobic side chain. Glycine is the simplest (R = -H), but has very little hydrophobic contribution. Proline has a cyclic side chain between the alpha-carbon and nitrogen (secondary amine, imino). Cyclic structure of proline makes it rigid and reduces structural flexibility of proteins containing proline.
- Glycine (G, Gly)
- Alanine (A, Ala)
- Proline (P, Pro)
- Valine (V, Val)
- Leucine (L, Leu)
- Isoleucine (I, Ile)
- Methionine (M, Met)
#### Aromatic R groups
Aromatic sidechain. Relatively hydrophobic. The hydroxyl of tyrosine can form hydrogen bonds and is important in some enzymes. Tyrosine and tryptophan are considerably more polar than phenylalanine due to hydroxyl and nitrogen respectively. The aromatic side chain can absorb UV light (280 nm), though less in phenylalanine. It follows that proteins containing aromatic amino acids strongly absorb UV light which can be measured. 
- Phenylalanine (F, Phe)
- Tyrosine (Y, Tyr)
- Tryptophan (W, Trp)
#### Polar, uncharged R groups
Hydrophilic, readily forms hydrogen bonds with water. Serine and threonine have hydroxyl groups. Asparagine and glutamine have amide groups. Cysteine is an exception, it has a thiol/sulfhydryl which is modestly polar and a weak acid. It can form weak hydrogen bonds with oxygen and nitrogen. Asparagine and glutamine are the amide derivatives of aspartic acid and glutamic acid.
The thiol in cysteine can form covalent disulfide bonds with other cysteines. The disulfide bonds is highly hydrophobic and play a role in forming covalent links in between polypeptide chains, either of the same or two different ones. 
- Serine (S, Ser)
- Threonine (T, Thr)
- Cysteine (C, Cys)
- Asparagine (N, Asn)
- Glutamine (Q, Gln)
#### Charged R groups
Most hydrophilic, has acid/base chemistry
##### Positive
Nitrogen-containing side chain. Order most basic to least basic: lysine, primary amino group; arginine, guanidinium group; histidine, aromatic imidazole group. 
Histidine's side chain has pKa near neutral, making it neutral or positive at pH 7. Histidine facilitates many enzyme-catalyzed reactions by acting as proton donator/acceptor.
- Lysine (K, Lys)
- Arginine (R, Arg)
- Histidine (H, His)
##### Negative
Carboxylic acid in its side chain. Net negative at pH 7.
- Aspartate/Aspartic acid (D, Asp)
- Glutamate/Glutamic acid (E, Glu)
#### Uncommon
- Selenocysteine (synthesized from serine)
- Pyrrolysine (occurs in methanogenic bacteria)
#### Acid/Base chemistry
Amino acids have capacity to act as both acid and base (amphoteric). Those without ionizable side chain will form zwitterions in neutral water solutions. This leads to a molecule with opposite charge but a net neutral charge. 
Having multiple ionizable groups mean they experience a buffering effect around their respective pKa:s. 
Another important property is the relation between the pH and pI, the isoelectric point/pH. pI represents the pH where all of the amino acid exists in its dipolar/zwitterionic form, and thus have *net* charge 0. If pH > pI, the amino acid is net negative and will flow towards the anode in an electric field. If pH < pI, it is positive and will follow the cathode.
