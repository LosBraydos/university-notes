#### Peptides and Proteins
Amino acids polymerize via condensation into peptides forming a peptide bond (amide bond). A peptide will have a N-terminus and a C-terminus which are the free alpha-amino respective carboxylic group of the terminal amino acid residues. The alpha-amino/carboxylic groups of non-terminal residues are chemically modified in a peptide and lose their acid-base properties.
Peptides are usually divided into dipeptides, oligopeptides (2-20 residues), and polypeptides (>20 residues).

## Structure
Proteins have four levels of structure which determines its ultimate shape and function. This is influenced by the amino acid sequence.
#### Primary
The amino acid sequence is the protein's **primary structure**. The chemical and structural properties of the residue side chains and their sequence determines the shape and function of the protein. Through noncovalent interactions and forces, mediated by side chain properties, certain spatial configurations are favored. 
#### Secondary
The structural and chemical properties of the residue side chains and their sequence affects how the protein will fold into its **secondary structure**. The amide bond has a planar structure due to its pi-bond characteristics. This makes the amide/peptide bond itself very rigid. The sigma bonds around the peptide bond can rotate freely and makes it possible for the protein to fold.![[Pasted image 20260428114449.png]]
These sigma bonds are called psi and phi, for the alpha-C to C bond and the N to alpha-C bond respectively. When two peptide groups are on the same plane to each other, the phi and psi angles are 180 degrees. Both phi and psi can have any angle - 180 deg < v < +180 deg, but the possible combinations are restricted by the steric effects of the residue side chains. 
When psi and phi remains the same or nearly the same throughout a segment, it creates what is known as a *regular* secondary structure. The two most prominent types are alpha helix and beta sheets
##### Alpha helix
Alpha helices exists commonly in the right handed form. Left handed helices are less stable and are not observed in proteins. Alpha helices are usually between 10-20 residues and can be up to 40. The residues are oriented so the side chains point outward. They are held together by hydrogen bonds between the electronegative C=O of aa1 and the electropositive NH of aa5.
The length between each full turn is 3.6 residues or 5.4 Å.
Helix stability is dependent on the properties of the amino acids. Smaller amino acids like alanine give good stability while rigid amino acids like proline destabilize the helix. However, glycine does not normally form alpha helices due to being too flexible. Chemical properties of the side chain also affect stability. A long sequence of Glu will destabilize at pH 7 due to the negative side chain repelling each other.
